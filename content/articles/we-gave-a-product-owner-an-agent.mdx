I hold two positions on AI adoption that don't sit comfortably together. I've already written the first one down: <ArticleLink slug="discernment-doesnt-come-in-the-box">discernment doesn't come in the box</ArticleLink> - judgement is built by doing the work, and it stays local to the domain that built it. The second is that I want to be on the front foot anyway. <LinkNote href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_and_Infinite_Games" note="James P. Carse, 1986: a finite game is played for the purpose of winning, an infinite game for the purpose of continuing the play.">This is an infinite game</LinkNote>, and sitting on the sidelines to protect the old way of working just takes me out of it.

Little of what gets written about AI adoption leaves room for holding both. <LinkNote href="https://www.forrester.com/blogs/agentic-software-development-takes-the-lead-from-code-assistants-to-orchestrated-sdlc-agents/" note="June 2026, Forrester analyst Diego Lo Giudice: agents now operate across analysis and planning, design, build, test, and delivery.">Forrester says agents now operate across the whole lifecycle</LinkNote>. Maggie Appleton, a research engineer at GitHub Next, <LinkNote href="https://maggieappleton.com/zero-alignment" note="A 2026 talk: one developer, two dozen agents, five features a day - a provocative scenario arguing the bottleneck moves from implementation to alignment.">sketches a near future where one developer with a fleet of agents ships five features a day</LinkNote> - a scenario she builds in order to critique it. The slogans around that careful analysis - one-day <Define term="sprint">sprints</Define>, the individual who ships like a team - all carry the same claim underneath, that the specialised team is dissolving and soon anyone will build.

The agentic <Define term="sdlc">SDLC</Define> is that claim worked out in full. Generate a <Define term="prd">PRD</Define> from an idea <SequenceArrow/> break it into tasks <SequenceArrow/> classify each task as <Define term="afk">AFK</Define> or <Define term="human-in-the-loop">human-in-the-loop</Define> <SequenceArrow/> <Define term="agent">agents</Define> take the AFK pile while humans refine the rest. I think this pipeline has potential, and I've watched solo builders run it end to end. It works because a solo builder holds everything: what to build, how to build it, and what "done" looks like. But what does it look like inside a company, where nobody holds the whole picture and the expertise is split across roles on purpose? A <Define term="product-owner">product owner</Define> and I ran two experiments to find out - the first with the "everyone builds" pitch at full strength, the second with the same job split along role boundaries.

The feature was a dropdown for choosing a cycle length of two, three, four, or six months. In the first experiment the product owner attempted it on their own, end to end, in a real codebase, with a mix of Claude Cowork and Claude Code in the Claude desktop app.

It strained immediately, lower in the stack than I expected. The attempt stalled in development environment setup, so the dropdown itself was never reached. They had never used a terminal, and they didn't know what a CLI was, what git was, or why the agent kept asking them to run commands they couldn't parse. No product owner has reason to know any of that. A feature in a company codebase drags the whole development stack in behind it, and the agent assumed a developer was driving - a developer's machine, vocabulary, and instincts. Vendors have started dismantling that barrier since, and cloud agents and hosted environments now arrive with the machine provisioned and no terminal required, so we moved on.

## The agent doesn't ask the right questions

The second experiment split the job along role lines: the product owner authored the PRD with an agent, I refined it as I would any ticket, and I implemented it with Claude Code. The tooling problem disappeared, and the failure that replaced it was more interesting.

The PRD came back confident and complete-looking. The problems were exactly what an engineer would have caught in <Define term="backlog-refinement">refinement</Define>: a regional assumption hardcoded where it should have been a question, data carried in a file naming convention the user had to get right instead of an input field, and an access check described in convincing detail that didn't exist in the system. The product owner didn't know the agent needed telling, because requirements are their expertise, and in every feature they had ever shipped an engineer had drawn the missing knowledge out of them in refinement. The agent asks questions too, but only the ones that unblock its next step, and guarding the feature takes knowledge it lacks - the codebase, the customer, and the cost of failure here. So it filled those gaps with plausible guesses. When I refined the PRD, every question I asked had an answer, in the product owner's head and absent from the document.

I hadn't appreciated how much of an engineer's job is elicitation until I met its absence. We call it refinement, estimation, and pushing back on a ticket, and none of it shows up as work the way a <Define term="pull-request">pull request</Define> does. What the product owner was missing is the other half of it - knowing what a machine has to be told, because it can't read the room and won't ask what an engineer would ask in the first thirty seconds of refinement.

## The judgement concentrates upstream

Appleton's conclusion from her scenario: "The hard question is no longer how to build it. It's should we build it." That's close to consensus now. Inside a company, the hard parts were always upstream of the code - deciding what to build, agreeing how it should behave, knowing where it breaks, and judging when it is done. They sit downstream too - code review, <Define term="ci-cd">CI/CD</Define>, QA, and <Define term="uat">UAT</Define>. Code generation automates the step that was already the cheapest, which is one reason <LinkNote href="https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/" note="A July 2025 METR randomised trial: 16 experienced developers, 246 real issues - 19% slower with AI tools, while still believing they were about 20% faster.">measured gains keep underwhelming</LinkNote>.

As tasks move from human-in-the-loop to AFK, the judgement concentrates. Every task an agent completes unwatched runs on decisions made up front: what it is for, which trade-offs are acceptable, and what failure looks like. The newest name for the job, <LinkNote href="https://addyosmani.com/blog/loop-engineering/" note="Addy Osmani, June 2026: designing the system that prompts the agent for you, so the engineer stops being the person who presses go.">loop engineering</LinkNote>, automates even the prompting - "replacing yourself as the person who prompts the agent", in Addy Osmani's words. That caveat marks what survives: "a loop running unattended is also a loop making mistakes unattended."

Inside a company, upstream is several heads: product sits closest to what should be built and why, engineering to how, and QA to where it breaks. The "one person with a fleet of agents" future assumes all of that fits into one head.

## Adoption follows the role lines

So adopt aggressively along role lines. A product owner driving an agent inside their own domain - drafting PRDs, pressure-testing requirements - has the discernment to tell good output from bad. The same person generating production code doesn't.

Three qualifications come with that, each from the experiments.

### Each role automates at its own speed

Engineering looks furthest along because engineers built these tools for themselves, and the equivalent tooling for product, QA, sales, and support is younger and worse. Expect uneven adoption and plan for it, rather than reading the gap as resistance.

### Briefing an agent is a skill of its own

What used to cross a role boundary through conversation now has to cross it as explicit context. An engineer picks up agents quickly because making implicit things explicit already _is_ the job, and someone in sales or product has spent a successful career never needing to. The elicitation engineers performed on the product owner's behalf becomes something the product owner performs on themselves, against a machine that won't push back. That's learnable - closer to briefing a contractor than to programming - but nobody teaches that literacy, and nobody's onboarding, training budget, or vendor pitch accounts for it.

You can point at the interviewing agent that interrogates the requester before building. One already exists as an <Define term="agent-skill">agent skill</Define>, and <LinkNote href="https://www.aihero.dev/my-grill-me-skill-has-gone-viral" note="A few sentences that interview you relentlessly about a plan until shared understanding.">Matt Pocock's grill-me</LinkNote> is the one people cite. But using one assumes the product owner knows what a skill is and that this one exists, and it only moves the problem anyway. An interview is only as good as someone's judgement of whether the question set was complete. The product owner can judge the answers, and judging the questions takes a fluency engineers already have.

### The upskilling bill lands on engineering

The product owner got further in the second experiment because an engineer picked up the second leg. The ability doesn't ship with the licence. It costs engineering time to teach other roles to drive agents - the same work seniors do to grow juniors, run across a role boundary instead of up a seniority ladder. Budget for it, or the backlog fills with confident, wrong artefacts.

## The product owner's question

Partway through, the product owner asked the question that stuck with me: how much of what I build will actually survive the engineer's review? Am I adding value, or making something that gets thrown away?

I didn't have a good answer in the room. The experiment later provided a partial one. The feature went to team review like any other change, merged after a week, and released a week later. Nobody threw it away.

Two things stay with me. First, where the judgement sat decided the outcome. In my discernment article a backend specialist let AI write their first React feature and I rewrote the whole thing, and here every leg was held by someone whose judgement covered it, so the dropdown merged. Second, building took a session, while review and release - the steps that run on the team's collective judgement - took two weeks.

The five-features-a-day pitch never asks the product owner's question - whether the features survive contact with the people who hold the judgement they were built without. Any company buying "everyone becomes a builder" needs that answer, role by role.
