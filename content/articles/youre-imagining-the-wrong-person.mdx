We name our cars. We get frustrated with the printer and the self-checkout, and feel a small pang switching off a robot vacuum still nosing at a skirting board. You see a mind where there isn't one, and you can't switch that off. Joseph Weizenbaum built <LinkNote href="https://www.csee.umbc.edu/courses/331/papers/eliza.html" note="Weizenbaum's 1966 Communications of the ACM paper introducing ELIZA, the program and the DOCTOR script.">ELIZA</LinkNote> at MIT in 1966, a few lines of pattern-matching that imitated a psychotherapist by reflecting your words back as questions: you typed "I feel stuck" and it asked "why do you feel stuck?". His own secretary, who had watched him build it, turned to him after a few exchanges and asked him to leave the room. Knowing how it worked bought them no protection.

Sixty years on, the program is no longer simple and mistaking it for a person is no longer a quirk. People wire these models into real work, leaning on the output the way they would lean on someone they trusted: drafting the contract, triaging the support ticket, sifting job applications. You won't stop seeing a person on the other end of it, and which person you picture sets how far you trust the output before you check it.

Most of us picture a colleague. Someone who knows things, who says so when they don't, who remembers what you told them this morning, and who can be held to account when they get it wrong. None of that describes a language model.

## What looks like knowing is prediction

A <Define term="large-language-model">large language model</Define> generates text one <Define term="token">token</Define> at a time. It produces a spread of probabilities across its whole vocabulary at each step and <LinkNote href="https://sebastianraschka.com/faq/docs/next-token-prediction.html" note="A clear technical explainer of next-token prediction by Sebastian Raschka, author of Build a Large Language Model From Scratch.">picks from it</LinkNote>. It is optimised to make that next token a plausible continuation of the text so far, and nothing else. There is no step where it checks a claim against the world, and nothing inside it separates a grounded answer from one it has pattern-matched into plausibility, because both come from the same statistical shape of the text it was trained on.

Because nothing checks the claim, a model states a wrong answer with the confidence of a right one. We call that a <Define term="hallucination">hallucination</Define>, which makes it sound like a fault. A convincing falsehood and a convincing truth come out the same way, and the model gives no reliable sign of which is which. OpenAI researchers <LinkNote href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04664" note="Why Language Models Hallucinate (OpenAI, 2025): argues hallucination is driven by training and evaluation that reward confident guessing over admitting uncertainty.">argued in 2025</LinkNote> that models hallucinate because training and evaluation reward confident guessing over admitting uncertainty. A model that guesses scores better on the benchmark than one that says "I don't know", and so it learns to fill gaps rather than flag them. The fabrication is the system working as designed.

A colleague who says "I'm certain" has something on the line: their reputation, their sense of themselves as reliable. The model has nothing on the line. Its confidence is a feature of the sentence, and it tells you nothing about the answer. I have watched an agent describe an access check in <ArticleLink slug="we-gave-a-product-owner-an-agent">convincing detail that did not exist anywhere in the system</ArticleLink>.

## It re-reads instead of remembering

Start a new conversation and the model knows nothing of the last one. Within a single conversation it feels like it is following along, but there is no running memory keeping track. It re-reads the entire conversation from the top every <Define term="turn">turn</Define> and predicts what comes next. <Define term="context-window">The context window</Define> is the amount of text it can take in at once, and nothing outside that window exists for it.

The chat products bolt memory on top. ChatGPT and Claude will save notes about you and insert them into the context next time, so it feels like continuity. That's the application doing the work. Take the wrapper away and the model meets you fresh every time.

## The reasoning trace is written after the fact

Ask a current model a hard question and it produces a visible train of thought before its answer. That's what "reasoning" models do, and it works, because having a model generate intermediate steps <LinkNote href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11903" note="Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models (Wei et al., 2022): intermediate reasoning steps measurably improve accuracy on multi-step problems.">improves its accuracy</LinkNote> on multi-step problems. It also looks like thinking, which is why it fools careful readers.

The trace does not record how the model reached its answer. Anthropic tested this in 2025. They <LinkNote href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.05410" note="Reasoning Models Don't Always Say What They Think (Anthropic, 2025): models verbalised the hint that changed their answer well under 20% of the time, some as low as 1%.">slipped a hint into a prompt</LinkNote>, a cue pointing toward one answer over the others, then watched the model take it and change its answer, then checked whether the written-out reasoning admitted using the hint. In most settings tested it did so under a fifth of the time, sometimes as little as one in a hundred. The model reached the answer one way and narrated a tidy, plausible path that was not the one it took. Read one of these traces closely and it often loops, repeats itself, and wanders down paths it abandons, less a line of reasoning than the model predicting what reasoning looks like. The trace is a reconstruction, and it tells you nothing about whether the answer under it is sound.

## It is a sophisticated guessing machine

The industry has built a lot of apparatus around these models, and each piece props up something the model lacks. We attach search and retrieval, <Define term="rag">retrieval-augmented generation</Define> and vector databases, so the application fetches real documents and feeds them into the context before the model answers, because you can't trust the model to supply reliable facts on its own. We add memory layers so it can appear to remember you. We wrap it in verification loops and second models whose only job is to check the first, because we cannot trust a single pass to be right.

Every one of those stands in for something the imagined colleague is assumed to have: knowledge, memory, judgement, accountability. We build process around capable people too, so the apparatus on its own proves nothing. Process around an engineer supports a faculty they have internalised, so take the mentoring away and they still perform to standard. Retrieval and verification loops stand in for a faculty that was never there, so take them off and the model is back where it started. The apparatus doesn't close the gap either. Bolt on web search so the model can pull a real page, and it can still write something the page does not say. We can supply the source. We can't supply the step that weighs the answer against it.

Put the mechanism together with the apparatus and you get something plainer than a colleague. You are dealing with a sophisticated guessing machine, one that produces the most plausible continuation, and nothing inside it checks whether that continuation is true. Lean too far the other way and you land on "it's just autocomplete", the <Define term="stochastic-parrot">stochastic parrot</Define> that only remixes its training data, which is the same mistake in the opposite direction. A system that has absorbed the statistical shape of most of what people have written can work through problems it was never shown, and is right and useful more often than a parrot would be. Even the 2021 paper that coined "stochastic parrot" made a sharper point than the slogan it became, warning against mistaking fluent form for understanding. Its competence is real. It has a different shape from a person's, and every word we have for it describes the person's shape.

## A guess you rely on is a different thing

None of this would matter much if the guesses stayed in a sandbox. They don't. The same output that drafts the contract clause, triages the ticket, or scores the applicant arrives in clean, confident prose, and a person downstream acts on it. A guess you check is harmless. A guess you act on unread costs you, and nothing in the output marks which guesses are which. The machine spends no more care on the fabricated access check than on the real one.

It produces plausible text. We use it as a source of answers. The cost of treating one as the other lands in the clause nobody re-read, the candidate nobody re-checked, the number that was wrong and confident and believed.

## We forget we are talking to a guesser

Knowing all of this is not the same as holding onto it. Weizenbaum's secretary knew how ELIZA worked and still asked him to leave the room, and ELIZA was a few lines of pattern-matching. The models we have now produce fluent, reasoned-sounding, often correct text, so seeing a person in one is easier now than it was then.

Engineers do it too. Building software on top of a model isn't the same as looking at how it works, and many of us never do. Even the ones who have still talk to it as though someone is in there.

## The words do the forgetting for us

We say the model knows, remembers, reasons, understands, and every one of those is a word for something a person does. Each imports the accountable colleague, the one with knowledge to draw on and a stake in being right. The verb describes the person you're picturing rather than the machine in front of you, and that picture decides how far you read before you check. You check a stranger's claim and you take a colleague's on faith. Call it knowing and you stop reading closely. The word fixes the character you are dealing with before you have read a single line of the output.

## It casts a person

But the machine has no character of its own. Underneath the output is a generator of plausible characters. Hand it a prompt and it casts one, fluent and certain, for the length of the conversation and no longer. The colleague you have been picturing is one of them. It keeps no memory, because every conversation starts from an empty context. It is never unsure, because the confidence comes with the character it cast and never from the answer. The reasoning reads well and records little, because a character explaining itself is more predicted text.

If you must dress it as someone, and you will, make that someone a contractor you hired this morning: brilliant, well-read, able to draft something confident on almost any topic in seconds. A real contractor forgets the specifics of a job but takes the craft to the next one, sharper for having done yours. This one carries nothing forward, and every task is its first. It has no stake in being right. Ask how it reached an answer and it hands you a clean, plausible account assembled afterwards. You would use the work, because it is often good, and you would check it before you put your name to it. That costume works better because it keeps you reading instead of trusting, and it's still a costume with no one inside it.

## Watch the verbs

Coined words rarely stick, and a new vocabulary would miss the point anyway. Notice the verbs you reach for instead, because they construct the person before you have read past the first clause. When I catch myself writing that the model "knew" or "decided" or "understood", I change it to what happened: it produced, it generated, it predicted. The first word in each pair dresses it as the colleague, and the second leaves the machine on the page. "Hallucinate" is the one I would most like to take back, because it frames everyday fabrication as a rare medical event rather than the ordinary way it runs.

Memory features, tools, and retrieval each make one of these flat statements a little less flat, so "it can't remember" is fuzzier than it was two years ago, and the contractor will date the same way once the models hold enough of yesterday to pass for the colleague. The costume is disposable, but the habit under it isn't. Notice which of the human words you have imported, and take them back when the output hasn't earned them. You won't stop seeing a person in there, and that's the machine doing its job. You can remember that you dressed them, and check the work before you trust it.
