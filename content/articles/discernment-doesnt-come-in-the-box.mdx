A senior engineer on my team shipped a React feature. They're excellent - years of deep backend work in .NET, the kind of developer whose code I rarely have to question - but they'd barely written any frontend, so they reached for AI and let it generate the React.

The <Define term="pull-request">pull request</Define> looked finished. It wasn't. The code used an <Define term="use-effect" code>effect</Define> to keep a copy of *derived state*: values you can compute from data you already hold, kept instead as a second copy that drifts out of sync. Heavy computations re-ran on every keystroke with no <Define term="memoisation">memoisation</Define>. And it was a few giant components holding tangles of internal state, where small focused pieces would have been clearer.

None of this is exotic. React's own documentation has a page titled <LinkNote href="https://react.dev/learn/you-might-not-need-an-effect" note="React's official guide to when an effect is unnecessary: it shows how to calculate values during render, memoise expensive work, and move logic into event handlers instead.">"You Might Not Need an Effect"</LinkNote> about the first two mistakes, and my team has written rules for each. I left review comments explaining every one, and they took those back to the AI and tried again, and the result was no better, because they couldn't tell whether the model's next attempt had fixed the problem or just moved it somewhere else.

They couldn't look at React and tell good from bad, and no amount of effort or seniority substitutes for that. I rewrote the feature from scratch.

Companies are making the same bet at scale. The pressure everywhere is to point AI at output metrics, and hitting those numbers means automating the work that grows engineers, so the damage never reaches this quarter's dashboard. It arrives years later, in the seniors you no longer have.

## Discernment doesn't transfer between domains

That senior wasn't being sloppy. They were working outside their domain, and the judgment that makes them excellent in .NET doesn't carry over into React.

Sort this by in-domain against out-of-domain work rather than junior against senior, and it stops being surprising, though most of the AI conversation sorts it the other way. An expert frontend developer would have generated the same bad React and caught it on the first read, and a backend expert can't, because spotting the mistake *is* the frontend expertise. You build that one domain at a time, and being good somewhere else doesn't hand it to you.

AI changes what each of them can do, but not by the same amount. It raises the *floor*, so anyone can produce code that compiles and roughly runs in a language they never learned. It doesn't raise the *ceiling*, the judgment of whether that code is any good, and that still has to be earned rather than generated.

## Plausible output is the kind that gets through

That gap is dangerous. AI lets people produce confident, plausible output well beyond their competence, and plausible is the failure mode that survives review, because obvious garbage gets thrown away and code that only looks right reaches a reviewer.

So the cost lands on whoever *can* judge the output, which means the seniors. Every out-of-domain AI submission that fails the sniff test is now a senior's problem to catch, explain, and often rewrite. AI was supposed to reduce the load on experienced engineers, and in my experience it's done the opposite, manufacturing more code that needs an expert eye and giving the author no way to pre-filter it.

A 2025 study from Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon, presented at the CHI human-computer interaction conference, points the same way. The researchers <LinkNote href="https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3706598.3713778" note="A CHI 2025 paper from Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon: a survey of knowledge workers finding that the more they trusted AI, the less critical thinking they applied to its output.">surveyed knowledge workers</LinkNote> about their AI use.

The more someone trusted the AI, the less critical thinking they applied to what it returned, and people leaned hardest on it, with the least scrutiny, exactly when they lacked the expertise to evaluate its output. Both findings held broadly across the group, and both are self-reported habits rather than measured behaviour.

## The work we automate is the work that makes seniors

Organisations deploy AI toward throughput: lines of code, pull requests, release frequency. Pick a number that looks like productivity, push AI at it, and AI will hit it. Optimise a measure that hard and it stops meaning anything, which is <Define term="goodharts-law">Goodhart's Law</Define>, now with something that hits any number you name.

Underneath that number sits a slower cost almost nobody prices in. The work we're most eager to automate - the boilerplate, the first stumbling implementation, the bug you stare at for an hour - is the *exact* work that turns a junior into a senior. You accumulate discernment rather than receive it, through reps: writing the wrong thing, feeling why it's wrong, getting feedback, trying again. That loop is the only path from floor to ceiling I've watched actually work, and it doesn't come in the box.

We've heard this before. Compilers would rot the discipline of managing memory, and Stack Overflow and the frameworks would raise a generation who couldn't reason from first principles, and each time the panic faded, because judgment relocated one level up the stack. AI differs in what sits under the abstraction. A compiler is deterministic and trustworthy, so you can stop checking its output and build on top of it, and AI is confidently wrong at random, so the layer never goes solid enough to stand on. It also automates the reps inside a domain rather than the domain itself: the junior writing React with AI never writes the wrong React and feels why, so the in-domain practice that would have built their discernment never happens.

Remember my senior's PR. I told them exactly what was wrong and it didn't help, because being told isn't the same as having learned. I've watched this with juniors for years: a senior points out the mistake, the junior nods, and three weeks later makes it again, because the correction was delivered rather than earned. That struggle is the learning, and it's what we're handing to the machine.

So an organisation optimising for output with AI dismantles its own pipeline while it games the metric. Today's seniors did, by hand, the work AI now does. Automate that work away for the next cohort and they never make the climb, and a few years later the people who could have reviewed the AI's output are the ones you forgot to grow.

## Cognitive debt comes due later

The same cost lands harder on one person. Researchers at the MIT Media Lab call it <Define term="cognitive-debt">cognitive debt</Define> in <LinkNote href="https://www.media.mit.edu/projects/your-brain-on-chatgpt/" note="A 2025 MIT Media Lab study (a preprint) that measured brain activity as people wrote essays with and without an LLM, reporting weaker neural engagement and recall among heavy AI users.">a 2025 study</LinkNote>, modelled on technical debt, the shortcut that costs more the longer you defer it. Leaning on an LLM saves effort now and charges interest later, in weaker memory, less original thinking, and a shallower grasp of work you only nominally produced.

People who wrote essays with an LLM showed the weakest brain engagement and the lowest sense of ownership, and 83% couldn't quote a single sentence from what they had *just* written. As the lead author, Nataliya Kosmyna, put it: "There is no cognitive credit card. You cannot pay this debt off."

That result is a preprint, with 54 participants and only 18 in the crucial session that switched people between the LLM and writing unaided, so I wouldn't lean on it alone. But it matches what I see on real teams, and it names something you'll recognise if you've ever shipped with AI and then couldn't maintain the result without it: you built it and didn't learn it, so when the tool is gone, so is your ability to keep it alive.

## Point AI at the engineer

The same tool that hollows out a junior when you aim it at an output metric can speed that junior's growth when you aim it at the junior.

I <ArticleLink slug="skills-that-teach">spent a month rewriting a skill</ArticleLink> and came out of it convinced that a good instruction *teaches* rather than dictates, transferring judgment instead of listing steps. The same thing holds one level up, so point AI at the engineer and its job is to transfer judgment rather than spare them the thinking that builds it.

For me that's meant a few habits. Use AI as a rubber duck, because explaining the problem to it surfaces the answer. Use it to attack your own design rather than defend it, and ask for the strongest case against the approach you've fallen in love with. Ask it *why* rather than *what*, over and over, so the reasoning lands with you and not just the code. And use it to generate learning material: before someone works in an unfamiliar domain, have AI build them a graded set of exercises against your own codebase, instead of doing that domain's work for them.

Whoever owns the metrics has something to change too. That PR looked finished and would have sailed through any throughput count while generating a pile of rework, so measure the rework: review-rejection rate, churn on recently shipped code, how often a change has to be redone. Those catch the cost that lines-per-day can't, and they're numbers you can chart.

## What I don't have figured out

Automating for throughput is sometimes simply correct, which is part of why organisations reach for the output metric. Plenty of boilerplate teaches nobody anything, a throwaway prototype deserves no ceremony, and a real deadline can outweigh a learning opportunity. So I automate freely when the code is disposable or the domain is already in the team's wheelhouse, and I protect the loop when someone is building durable judgment in a domain they'll own.

Growing someone costs more in exactly the dimensions a metrics culture punishes, because it's slower and it spends scarce senior time. The output metric wins by default because it's *legible*, whatever it actually measures.

A firm could buy seniors instead of growing them, letting AI cover the floor while it hires experience away, and that works for one company and fails for all of them at once, draining a senior pool already tightening for this reason. The next generation may also build a different discernment, steering AI rather than reading raw code, though that skill is still earned through reps and feedback, which an output-obsessed culture strips out either way. This is a forecast. Nobody has yet watched an AI-raised cohort fail to produce seniors, because not enough time has passed, so I'm reasoning from a mechanism and from my own teams rather than from a settled result.

My own prescription curdles the same way. "Use AI to grow your people" becomes a hollow metric the moment someone counts hours-mentored-by-AI, and you want discernment in the engineer, so nothing spares you the work of judging whether it's actually growing.

The same tool could speed the climb from junior to senior faster than anything before it, and I don't have the pipeline that reliably does that. It's the work worth doing, and I'd rather more of us were building it than racing to the bottom of a metric.
